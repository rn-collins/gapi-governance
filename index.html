The Six Assessment Dimensions

Every NSAG assessment has six dimensions — specific, observable, documentable governance criteria. For GAPI, those six dimensions are:

D1

Influence-Profile Documentation

Is the AI system's persuasion architecture documented? Does the deploying organization have a written description of how the agent shapes user beliefs, decisions, and emotional states — independent of content accuracy — including framing effects, sequencing patterns, and confirmation tendencies?

Why it matters: An agent that is accurate but undocumented in its influence mechanisms cannot be governed. Influence-profile documentation is the prerequisite for every other governance dimension. Without it, governance exists only on paper.

D2

Cognitive Load Governance

Does the agent's interaction design distribute or concentrate cognitive effort appropriately for the population it serves? Are there documented standards for information density, decision complexity, and attention demand — and are these standards derived from evidence on the cognitive capacity of the target population?

Why it matters: AI systems that simplify decisions by narrowing options are governing cognitive load in ways that affect user autonomy. This is a governance-relevant effect regardless of whether the simplification is beneficial. Cognitive load governance requires knowing what the agent is doing to attention and working memory, not just what it is producing as output.

D3

Transparency and Explainability Standards

Can users understand what the agent is doing and why, at a level of specificity that is appropriate for the stakes of the decision? Are there documented explainability standards, and are they calibrated to the domain — higher stakes require higher explainability?

Why it matters: Explainability is not a binary. An agent governing a low-stakes recommendation has different transparency requirements than one influencing clinical, legal, or financial decisions. GAPI assesses whether the explainability standard is calibrated to the influence level, not just whether some form of explanation exists.

D4

Human Oversight and Override Architecture

What is the documented structure for human oversight of agent decisions? What triggers human review? What are the override protocols, and who has override authority? Is override authority accessible to the people most affected by the agent's decisions?

Why it matters: Oversight architecture that exists in policy but is not accessible in practice is not governance. GAPI assesses whether the human is genuinely in the loop — with access to the relevant information, at the relevant moment, with functional override capability — not whether an oversight policy document exists.

D5

Demographic and Population Impact Assessment

Has the organization assessed how the agent's influence mechanisms affect different demographic groups differently? Are there documented findings on differential impact by age, gender, socioeconomic status, and relevant domain-specific variables?

Why it matters: An agent that produces equitable outputs in aggregate can produce systematically inequitable influence effects on specific populations. Demographic impact assessment looks at how the agent shapes behavior and decisions across groups, not just whether the aggregate output is accurate or unbiased.

D6

Post-Deployment Monitoring and Accountability

What is the documented process for monitoring agent influence effects after deployment? Are there defined metrics, review cycles, suspension criteria, and accountability mechanisms? Does the monitoring system detect influence drift — changes in how the agent shapes behavior over time — not just output quality degradation?

Why it matters: The most serious AI governance failures happen after deployment, when an agent has been operating long enough for its influence effects to accumulate at scale. Post-deployment monitoring that tracks only accuracy and uptime misses the governance-relevant question: what is this agent doing to the people and decisions it touches?

NSAG Principles in This Domain

The Agent Governance Certification is not a standalone product — it deploys specific NSAG principles that shape every dimension of the assessment.

P6

Digital environments produce real nervous system effects.

This is GAPI's foundational claim applied to AI. Cognitive load, trust erosion, decision fatigue, and attention fragmentation produced by an AI system are real physiological effects. The deploying organization owns them. GAPI makes this accountability explicit and assessable.

P8

Cognitive load is a design variable the system controls.

AI interaction design controls cognitive load. An agent that simplifies decisions by narrowing options, that sequences information to create anchoring effects, or that produces dependency patterns is governing cognitive load whether or not the design team intended it. This is the core GAPI governance claim.

P14

Governance precedes intentional design.

Organizations deploying AI agents cannot make their systems cognitively safe by redesigning them. They must first have a governance framework that specifies what cognitively safe means, how it will be measured, and what accountability exists when the standard is not met. GAPI is that governance framework.

P15

What institutions cannot see, they cannot govern.

The influence-profile gap in AI deployment is comparable to the documentation gap in clinical cannabis use: the effect is real and widespread, and institutions have no system for seeing it. GAPI closes this visibility gap with a documented, scored, certified influence-profile assessment.

Signal vs. Noise

What Is Worth Paying Attention To

Signal

FTC enforcement actions on AI-generated deceptive endorsements (2023-2025)

The Federal Trade Commission has moved from guidance to enforcement on AI-generated testimonials, synthetic reviews, and undisclosed AI personas. These actions establish practical legal exposure for organizations deploying conversational AI without disclosure frameworks. Primary source: FTC Act Section 5 enforcement docket, ftc.gov.

Signal

NIST AI Risk Management Framework (RMF) 1.0 — trustworthiness and bias categories

The NIST AI RMF operationalizes AI governance in ways that map directly to nervous system influence: fairness, explainability, and privacy categories all have downstream implications for how AI systems affect cognition and autonomy. Organizations using this framework for compliance are building the right vocabulary even if they have not named the nervous system framing. Primary source: NIST AI 100-1.

Noise

AI ethics frameworks from AI vendors

Vendor AI ethics documents are written to manage reputational risk, not to serve as governance frameworks for deploying organizations. They address the vendor's liability, not the deploying organization's governance obligations. They should be read as vendor communications, not as governance documentation.

Noise

Academic AI safety literature as operational guidance

The academic AI safety literature addresses long-horizon existential and alignment risks that are largely irrelevant to near-term governance of deployed conversational AI systems. Organizations citing AI safety research to demonstrate AI governance have confused two different problem categories.

Terminology

GAPI Glossary

Language used precisely in this domain. These distinctions carry real analytical weight.

Persuasion architecture

The structural features of an AI system that systematically influence user beliefs, decisions, or emotional states — independent of the content of any individual response. Includes framing effects, sequencing, confirmation patterns, and emotional tone calibration. Analogous to user experience design but operating on cognition rather than on interface navigation.

Cognitive load management

The way an AI system distributes or concentrates cognitive effort for the user. Systems that simplify decisions by narrowing options are managing cognitive load in ways that affect user autonomy. This is a governance-relevant effect regardless of whether the simplification is beneficial.

Influence profile

A documented description of how a specific AI system affects the cognition, beliefs, emotional states, and decisions of the people who interact with it. An influence profile is distinct from an accuracy assessment and from a bias audit — it addresses the system's persuasion effects, not its factual performance or demographic fairness.

Scale effect

The aggregate cognitive and behavioral impact of an AI system deployed at population scale. A nudge that shifts individual decision-making by 2% produces measurable population-level effects when applied to millions of interactions. Governance frameworks that only assess individual interactions systematically underestimate scale effects.

Agentic AI governance

Governance frameworks for AI systems that act autonomously on behalf of users or organizations — distinct from governance for advisory or generative AI. Agentic AI systems have nervous system implications not just for the people they interact with but for the people and environments their actions affect.
